01

What is appeasement?

Appeasement is the name historians give to the foreign policy pursued by Britain and France towards Nazi Germany during the 1930s, especially under Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain. The idea was simple: rather than confronting Hitler over his demands, Britain and France would give in to reasonable-sounding requests to keep the peace, on the assumption that Hitler had limited aims and could be satisfied.

Why did Britain and France appease Hitler?

  • Memory of the First World War. A generation had been killed in the trenches. The public, politicians and press were desperate to avoid another war.
  • Military unpreparedness. Britain had disarmed heavily after 1918 and was only beginning to rearm in 1936–39.
  • Economic pressure. The Great Depression had cut defence budgets and public appetite for war spending.
  • Guilt about Versailles. Many in Britain believed the Treaty of Versailles had been unfair on Germany. Letting Hitler reverse parts of it seemed reasonable.
  • Fear of communism. A strong Germany was seen as a useful buffer against the Soviet Union.
  • Miscalculation. Chamberlain believed Hitler was a rational statesman who would keep his word. He was wrong.
Exam pattern to remember

Every appeasement source you see will be arguing for appeasement (it keeps the peace), against appeasement (it rewards aggression), or reflecting on it afterwards (it failed and led to war). Identifying which of these three positions your source takes is the fastest way into the message.

1938: the key year

The homework sheet has a timeline on it that has been scribbled out. Here it is again, with the important events. Both Sources C and D sit inside this story.

13 March 1938
Anschluss. Germany annexes Austria. Britain and France do not act. Hitler has now broken the Treaty of Versailles in plain sight.
Late April 1938
Daladier warns the British that Hitler's real aim is the domination of Europe and that Czechoslovakia, Poland and Romania will follow. This is Source D.
Summer 1938
Hitler demands the Sudetenland, the German-speaking border region of Czechoslovakia. Sudeten Nazis stir up unrest.
15 September 1938
Chamberlain flies to Berchtesgaden to meet Hitler. Hitler demands the Sudetenland.
22 September 1938
Chamberlain meets Hitler again at Bad Godesberg. Hitler's demands have grown.
29–30 September 1938
The Munich Agreement. Chamberlain, Daladier, Hitler and Mussolini meet in Munich. Czechoslovakia is not invited. The Sudetenland is handed over to Germany. Chamberlain returns to London claiming “peace for our time.”
15 March 1939
Appeasement fails. Hitler invades the rest of Czechoslovakia. Britain and France now realise the policy has failed. Daladier’s warning from April 1938 has been proved right.
1 September 1939
Germany invades Poland. Two days later, Britain and France declare war.
Date trap to avoid

Do not reach for Manchuria (1931) in this homework. Manchuria is a League of Nations / 1930s crisis, not an appeasement source. Keep the focus on 1938, with supporting references to the Anschluss (March 1938) and Munich (September 1938).

03

How to analyse a source

The teacher has given four questions. These are the standard AQA source questions, so getting the structure right here will pay off in the real exam too.

1
What does the source show or tell us? Give a short quote or directly describe what is shown. This is the content.
2
What is the overall message? This is not just what is shown. It is the argument the source is making. Ask: what does the creator want me to think?
3
How useful is the provenance? Provenance means: who made it, when, where, for what audience and why. Useful for what purpose? Honest but biased is still useful; reliable but vague is less useful.
4
What is happening at this time? Place the source on the timeline. Dates are essential. If you cannot say what was going on in the wider world when the source was made, you have not done question 4.
The link that lifts the grade

After every point, connect the source to the wider story. A Grade 5 answer describes the source. A Grade 8 answer explains how the source fits into the argument about whether appeasement worked. Always link back to the events on the timeline.

Source C: Dr Seuss, “I Was Weak and Run-Down”

SOURCE C

The source at a glance

An American political cartoon in the style of a patent-medicine advertisement. Two eagle-like birds in Uncle Sam top hats. The “before” bird is weak, drooping and has “a foul case of appeasement.” The “after” bird is upright and muscled after taking a tonic called “GUTS,” strong enough to “punch Mister Hitler right in the snoot.” The cartoon is signed “Dr Seuss.”

What the source shows (Q1)

The cartoon mocks appeasement as an illness. Describe the before/after structure and the phrase “a foul case of Appeasement”. Note the Uncle Sam hats, which mark the birds as representing America. Note the aggressive final line: “punch Mister Hitler right in the snoot.”

The overall message (Q2)

Dr Seuss is arguing that appeasement is a sickness that weakens America, and that the cure is strength, courage and a willingness to confront Hitler directly. The cartoon attacks American isolationism and appeasement-style thinking and calls for a tougher stance against Nazi Germany.

The message is a clear anti-appeasement argument: only courage, not compromise, will stop Hitler.

Provenance and usefulness (Q3)

Who made it

Theodor Seuss Geisel, known as Dr Seuss. An American cartoonist who drew political cartoons for the New York newspaper PM during the early 1940s, before becoming famous for children’s books.

When

Early 1940s, during the period when America was debating whether to enter the war. Most Dr Seuss war cartoons were published between 1941 and 1943.

Audience and purpose

American newspaper readers. Designed to persuade Americans to abandon isolationism and support action against Hitler. It is satire, not reporting.

What it is useful for

Very useful for showing how appeasement was viewed critically at the time by those who wanted America to fight. Less useful as a factual account of what appeasement actually achieved.

Model provenance sentence (Grade 8 language)

This source is useful because it shows how appeasement was being attacked as cowardice in the American press in the early 1940s. However, it is a satirical cartoon by a cartoonist known for his anti-isolationist views, so it is useful for showing opinion and propaganda rather than an objective account of appeasement’s results. Its usefulness lies precisely in its bias: it captures the mood turning against appeasement.

What is happening at this time (Q4)

By the early 1940s, appeasement had been fully discredited. Hitler had already invaded Czechoslovakia (March 1939), Poland (September 1939), France (May 1940) and was bombing Britain. America was still officially neutral but public opinion was shifting. The cartoon belongs to the wider argument in America about whether to stay out of the war or fight Hitler. Dr Seuss was firmly on the anti-Hitler side.

Two-minute talking points for Source C
  1. Describe the cartoon: before and after, the birds with Uncle Sam hats, the fake-advert style, the key phrases.
  2. State the message: appeasement is a disease, strength is the cure, America must be ready to hit Hitler.
  3. Explain the provenance: Dr Seuss, American, early 1940s, satirical newspaper cartoon aimed at persuading Americans.
  4. Judge its usefulness: very useful for showing the anti-appeasement mood, less useful as factual history.
  5. Place it in time: after Munich had already failed, when the consequences of appeasement were becoming clear.

Source D: Daladier, late April 1938

SOURCE D

The source at a glance

A primary extract from Édouard Daladier, the French Prime Minister, speaking to the British in late April 1938. Daladier warns that Hitler aims at “a domination of the continent in comparison with which the ambitions of Napoleon were feeble,” and predicts that after Czechoslovakia will come Poland, then Romania, then the West. He argues that Britain and France must stand firm and safeguard Czechoslovakia, or they will only “precipitate the war they wish to avoid.”

What the source shows (Q1)

Daladier is telling Britain that Hitler’s aims are not limited. He uses the Napoleon comparison to stress the scale of Hitler’s ambitions. He lists the next likely victims in order: Czechoslovakia, Poland, Romania, the West. He warns that giving in (capitulating) will not prevent war, it will cause war.

The overall message (Q2)

Daladier is making an anti-appeasement argument from inside the French government. The message is that Hitler will not stop at one concession, so concessions will not buy peace. He is calling on Britain to stand with France in defending Czechoslovakia.

“If... the Western Powers capitulate again they will only precipitate the war they wish to avoid.” Daladier to the British, late April 1938

Provenance and usefulness (Q3)

Who made it

Édouard Daladier, French Prime Minister. Head of the French government and one of the people who would sign the Munich Agreement five months later.

When

Late April 1938. After the Anschluss, before the Sudeten crisis fully boiled over, several months before Munich.

Audience and purpose

A private warning to the British government. Not a speech to the public. The purpose is to persuade Britain to resist Hitler rather than appease him.

What it is useful for

Very useful. It shows that at least one senior Western leader saw through Hitler’s aims before Munich. It refutes any claim that “nobody could have predicted” what Hitler would do.

The twist to mention

Daladier warned against appeasement in April 1938, yet in September 1938 he personally signed the Munich Agreement, handing the Sudetenland to Hitler. This gap between what he said and what he did is worth pointing out in your talk. It shows how powerful the pressure to appease was, even on those who knew better.

Model provenance sentence (Grade 8 language)

This source is highly useful because it comes from the French Prime Minister himself in April 1938, which is contemporary with the crisis and from the top of the French government. It shows Western leaders were warned of Hitler’s full ambitions before Munich. However, Daladier went on to sign the Munich Agreement anyway, which suggests that even those who saw the danger felt politically unable to act. The usefulness of this source is that it destroys the later excuse that Hitler’s plans were unknowable.

What is happening at this time (Q4)

Late April 1938 sits between two moments. The Anschluss had just happened in March 1938, showing Hitler was willing to break Versailles and swallow a whole country with no Western response. Ahead lay the summer Sudeten crisis and the Munich conference in September. Daladier is speaking at exactly the point when Britain and France had to decide whether the Anschluss was a one-off or part of a pattern. Daladier’s answer was clear: it was a pattern, and the pattern would lead to war.

Two-minute talking points for Source D
  1. Introduce: Édouard Daladier, French Prime Minister, late April 1938, private warning to Britain.
  2. Quote the Napoleon line and the list of countries to show Daladier saw Hitler’s ambition clearly.
  3. State the message: appeasement will not prevent war, it will cause it. Britain and France must stand firm.
  4. Judge provenance: extremely useful because it is a primary source from a head of government at the time of the crisis.
  5. Add the twist: Daladier later signed Munich anyway, showing how strong the pull of appeasement was.
  6. Place in time: after the Anschluss (March 1938), before Munich (September 1938). The warning was not heeded, and by 1939 it had been proved right.
06

How to structure a two-minute talk

Two minutes is roughly 250 to 300 spoken words. That is tight. The structure below will keep the talk focused and cover everything the teacher has asked for.

A
Opening (20 seconds) Introduce the source in one sentence. Say what it is, who made it, and when.
B
Content (30 seconds) Describe what is shown. Include one short direct quote or a clear description of a specific detail.
C
Message (30 seconds) Say clearly what the source is arguing and who it is trying to persuade.
D
Provenance and context (30 seconds) Say when it sits on the timeline, and judge how useful it is and for what purpose.
E
Closing judgement (10 seconds) One sentence tying the source to the bigger story of appeasement.
Delivery tip

Write each section as a single sentence on a cue card. Five cue cards, one per section. Do not write out the full speech, because it will sound read-aloud. Bullet points on a card force natural speech.

Phrase bank for source analysis

These are the sentence openings the teacher is looking for. Use four or more across the two answers.

For the message

  • The overall message of this source is that...
  • The creator is arguing that...
  • The source is trying to persuade the audience to...
  • At its core, this source attacks the idea that...

For provenance

  • This source is useful because it comes from...
  • However, the source is limited because...
  • Its usefulness lies in what it reveals about opinion at the time, rather than...
  • Because the creator was a..., the source tells us more about... than about...

For context

  • At the time this source was produced, Hitler had just...
  • This source sits between two key events:... and...
  • This needs to be read against the background of...
  • The events that followed proved the source right or wrong, because...

For the closing judgement

  • Ultimately, this source shows that appeasement was seen as...
  • Taken together with the events of 1938 and 1939, this source helps explain why...
  • This is why the source is important to the story of appeasement:...
